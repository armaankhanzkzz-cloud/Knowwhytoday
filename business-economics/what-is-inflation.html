What Is Inflation?

Business & Economics

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services, which reduces the purchasing power of money.

What does it actually mean for prices to rise?

Inflation isn't about one item getting pricier, like a coffee shop raising the cost of a latte. It's about the broad, sustained upward drift of prices across an economy, so that the same amount of money buys a little less over time. A dollar, pound, or rupee that could buy a certain basket of groceries a year ago typically buys less of that same basket today. This is usually described as a loss of purchasing power. Some inflation is considered a normal, even healthy, feature of a growing economy, but when it runs too fast for too long, it starts to squeeze household budgets, savings, and the overall stability people expect from the money they hold.

What actually causes inflation?

Economists generally point to a few main drivers. Demand-pull inflation happens when demand for goods and services outpaces what the economy can supply, so prices get bid upward. Cost-push inflation happens from the other direction, when the cost of producing things rises, such as pricier raw materials, wages, or energy, and businesses pass that expense on to customers. There's also built-in inflation, where workers expect prices to keep climbing and push for higher wages to keep up, which then feeds back into higher production costs and, in turn, higher prices. In practice, these forces often overlap, and a single stretch of inflation is rarely caused by just one of them alone.

How do economists actually measure it?

The most widely used tool is the Consumer Price Index, or CPI, which tracks how much a fixed basket of everyday goods and services costs over time. This basket includes things like food, housing, transportation, and medical care, weighted according to how much of the average household's spending actually goes toward each category. Statistical agencies, such as the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, collect prices on tens of thousands of items each month and compare them to previous periods to calculate a percentage change. Economists also look at core inflation, which excludes food and energy prices because they tend to be relatively volatile, making core inflation useful for assessing underlying price trends.

Is inflation always a bad thing?

Not inherently. A small, steady rate of inflation, often around 2 percent a year in many advanced economies, is generally viewed as consistent with price stability and is a target that many central banks aim for. Problems tend to show up at the extremes. High inflation erodes savings and makes planning difficult, since prices and costs become harder to predict. At the other end, deflation, meaning falling prices, sounds appealing on the surface but can actually be damaging too, since it often discourages spending, as people wait for prices to drop further, which can slow the entire economy down. What matters most is stability and predictability, not just which direction prices happen to be moving.

Who ends up dealing with the effects of inflation?

Almost everyone, though not equally. People on fixed incomes, such as retirees whose pensions do not adjust fully with inflation, can be particularly vulnerable, since their income may not rise alongside prices. Savers holding cash also lose ground, since money sitting still loses value while prices climb around it. Borrowers can sometimes benefit slightly, since they're repaying loans with money that's worth a little less than when they borrowed it. Governments and central banks respond to high inflation using tools like interest rate changes, since raising rates tends to cool spending and borrowing, which can help bring price growth back down over time.

In short

Inflation is the gradual, broad-based rise in prices that reduces how much a given amount of money can buy. It stems from a mix of causes, including strong demand, rising production costs, and expectations that feed on themselves, and it's tracked mainly through tools like the Consumer Price Index. A modest, steady rate of inflation is generally considered normal and even useful for a functioning economy, but both runaway inflation and outright deflation bring their own problems. Its effects also aren't spread evenly, landing hardest on savers and people with fixed incomes, which is part of why managing it carefully remains one of the central jobs of economic policy.
