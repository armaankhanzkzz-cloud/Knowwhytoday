What Is Opportunity Cost?

Business & Economics

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative that is forgone when a choice is made.

What does "forgone alternative" actually mean here?

Every choice closes off other choices, and opportunity cost is simply a way of putting a value on what gets closed off. If someone spends a Saturday afternoon at the cinema instead of working a paid shift, the opportunity cost isn't the price of the movie ticket, it's the wages they didn't earn during that time. The same logic scales up to businesses and governments. A factory that uses its warehouse space to store finished goods gives up whatever else that space could have been used for, such as renting it out. Because resources like time, money, and space are always limited, virtually every decision carries some version of this trade-off, whether or not anyone stops to calculate it.

Is opportunity cost the same as the price tag on something?

Not quite, and this is where the concept gets more useful than a simple cost comparison. Economists split costs into two categories: explicit costs, which involve an actual outlay of money, like paying tuition or buying materials, and implicit costs, which don't involve spending cash but still represent something given up, such as the salary someone could have earned instead of running their own business. Opportunity cost captures both. A student's opportunity cost of attending university, for example, isn't just tuition and books, it also includes the income they could have made working full-time instead. Leaving out that implicit piece tends to make a decision look cheaper than it really is.

Why do economists treat this as such a central idea?

Opportunity cost sits at the core of how economists think about scarcity, since resources like money, labour, and time are always limited relative to everything people might want to do with them. Because of that, every decision, whether it's made by a household, a business, or a government, is really a choice between competing uses of the same limited resources. Thinking in terms of opportunity cost forces a comparison that a simple budget doesn't capture on its own: not just "can I afford this," but "what am I giving up by choosing this over the next-best option." That framing is a big part of why the concept shows up constantly in economics, from personal finance to national policy.

How is this different from a sunk cost?

The two are often confused, but they point in opposite directions. Opportunity cost is forward-looking, weighing what a choice will cost in terms of the alternative being given up. A sunk cost is backward-looking, referring to money or effort already spent that can't be recovered no matter what happens next. A common mistake, sometimes called the sunk cost fallacy, is letting past spending influence a current decision, such as continuing to fund a failing project purely because so much has already been invested in it. Economists generally argue that sunk costs shouldn't factor into future decisions at all, while opportunity cost, since it's about what lies ahead, always should.

How does this play out in everyday decisions?

Once the concept clicks, it tends to show up everywhere. A business deciding whether to manufacture a product in-house or buy it from a supplier is weighing the opportunity cost of tying up its own factory capacity. A government deciding how to allocate a limited budget between healthcare and infrastructure is weighing the opportunity cost of funding one at the expense of the other. Even something as small as choosing how to spend a free evening involves an opportunity cost, since time spent one way can't also be spent another. Recognising this trade-off doesn't make decisions automatically easier, but it does make them more deliberate, since it puts the true cost of a choice on the table rather than hiding it.

In short

Opportunity cost is the value of whatever gets given up when one option is chosen over another, and it includes both explicit costs, which involve actual spending, and implicit costs, which don't. It's different from a sunk cost, since it looks forward at what a decision will cost rather than backward at what's already been spent. Because resources are always limited, the concept underpins how economists analyse choices at every scale, from an individual deciding how to spend an afternoon to a government allocating its budget. Weighing the next-best alternative, rather than just the price tag in front of you, is what turns a simple choice into an informed one.
