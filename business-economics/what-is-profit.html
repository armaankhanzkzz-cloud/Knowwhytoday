What Is Profit?

Business & Economics

Profit is the amount remaining when total revenue exceeds total costs. It is an important measure of a business’s financial performance.

What does profit actually measure?

Profit is simply what's left over once a business has covered its costs. If a company brings in more money from sales than it spends producing and running the business, the difference is profit. If costs end up higher than revenue, the business has a loss instead. That basic idea sounds simple, but in practice profit gets broken down into a few different layers, because "how much a business made" can be measured at different stages, before or after certain costs get subtracted. Which figure matters most depends on what question someone is trying to answer.

What's the difference between gross, operating, and net profit?

Gross profit is the first and narrowest layer. It's total revenue minus the direct cost of producing whatever was sold, things like raw materials or the labour directly tied to making the product. It shows how much revenue remains after the direct costs of producing what was sold are deducted. Operating profit goes a step further, subtracting the everyday running costs of the business too, such as rent, salaries, and utilities, giving a picture of how efficiently the core business operates as a whole. Net profit is the final layer, the number left after every remaining expense is subtracted, including interest and taxes. This is usually the figure people mean when they talk about the "bottom line," since it reflects what the business actually kept after everything.

Is accounting profit the same as economic profit?

Not quite, and the difference matters more than it might seem. Accounting profit is the standard version, revenue minus explicit costs, the kind that show up on an income statement like wages, rent, and materials. Economic profit takes things further by also subtracting implicit costs, which are the opportunity costs of resources the business already owns, like the money a founder could have earned investing their savings elsewhere. A business can report a healthy accounting profit while its economic profit is actually negative, meaning the resources tied up in the business could have earned more somewhere else. Economists lean on this second version because it captures whether a business is genuinely the best use of its resources, not just whether it's covering its bills.

Why does profit matter to a business?

Profit is one of the clearest signals of whether a business model actually works. Consistent profit gives a business room to reinvest in itself, whether that's hiring, expanding, or improving its product, without having to rely entirely on outside funding to grow. It's also central to how investors and lenders evaluate a business, since a track record of profit is an important factor investors and lenders consider when assessing a business's financial strength and prospects. That said, profit isn't the only thing that determines whether a business is doing well. A company can be profitable and still run into serious trouble if it doesn't manage its cash flow carefully, since profit on paper and cash actually available aren't always the same thing at any given moment.

Do all organisations aim to maximise profit?

Most for-profit businesses treat maximising profit as a central goal, since it directly benefits the owners or shareholders who take on the financial risk of running the business. But that's not universal. Nonprofits and many social enterprises are structured around a mission rather than a financial return, and any surplus they generate typically gets reinvested into that mission instead of distributed to owners. Even among for-profit companies, some deliberately sacrifice short-term profit to invest heavily in growth or research, betting that a larger payoff will come later. Profit still matters to these organisations, since none of them can survive indefinitely while losing money, but it isn't always the single number they're optimising for.

In short

Profit is what remains after a business subtracts its costs from its revenue, and it can be measured at different levels, from gross profit at the simplest stage through to net profit once every expense is accounted for. Economists also draw a distinction between accounting profit and economic profit, since the second one factors in the cost of opportunities given up, not just money actually spent. Profit isn't the only measure of a healthy business, but it remains one of the clearest indicators of whether a business's underlying model is actually working.
