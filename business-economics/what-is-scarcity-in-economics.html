What Is Scarcity in Economics?

Business & Economics

Scarcity exists because resources are limited while human wants are extensive. It forces individuals, businesses, and governments to make choices.

Why does scarcity exist in the first place?

Scarcity comes down to a simple mismatch: the things people want are effectively endless, while the resources available to produce them, such as land, labour, raw materials, and time, are not. Even the wealthiest person or the richest country can't have everything, since there's always more that could be done with more time, more money, or more materials. This mismatch is often called the basic economic problem, and it's the starting point for economics as a field. Rather than asking whether scarcity can be eliminated, economics is largely about how individuals, businesses, and societies decide who gets what, given that not everyone's wants can be satisfied at once.

Is scarcity the same as a shortage?

No, and mixing the two up is a common source of confusion. A shortage is temporary, usually caused by a sudden spike in demand or a disruption in supply, like a store running out of a popular product during a sale. Scarcity is permanent and structural, existing regardless of how well supply and demand happen to be balanced at a given moment. Even when a store's shelves are fully stocked, the underlying resources used to make those products, such as raw materials, energy, and labour, are still limited. A shortage can be fixed by restocking; scarcity, by contrast, is simply a permanent condition of economic life that never fully goes away.

Are all scarce resources scarce in the same way?

Economists sometimes distinguish between relative scarcity and absolute scarcity. Relative scarcity describes a resource that exists in limited supply compared with how much people want it, such as beachfront property or tickets to a popular concert. Absolute scarcity refers to a physical limit on the total amount of a resource, such as the Earth’s finite land area. In introductory economics, however, scarcity generally refers to resources being limited relative to human wants.

What questions does scarcity force every economy to answer?

Because no economy can produce everything everyone wants, scarcity pushes every society, regardless of its size or system, to answer three core questions. What should be produced, given that resources used for one thing can't also be used for another? How should it be produced, meaning which methods and combinations of labour, capital, and materials are used to make it? And for whom should it be produced, meaning how are the resulting goods and services actually distributed among people? Different economic systems, from market economies to centrally planned ones, answer these questions in very different ways, but no system, no matter how it's organised, gets to skip answering them altogether.

How does scarcity connect to opportunity cost?

Scarcity is really the reason opportunity cost exists at all. If resources were unlimited, choosing one option would never require giving up another, so nothing would ever have to be sacrificed. Because resources are limited, though, every choice, from how a government spends its budget to how a household spends its evening, means forgoing some other use of that same time or money. This is why scarcity and opportunity cost are usually taught together: scarcity explains why choices have to be made at all, while opportunity cost explains what those choices actually cost once they're made.

In short

Scarcity is the basic economic condition in which limited resources can't fully satisfy unlimited human wants, and it's permanent, unlike a temporary shortage. It comes in two forms, relative scarcity, where more of something could theoretically exist but not enough to meet demand, and absolute scarcity, where a resource simply can't be increased at all. Because of scarcity, every economy has to decide what to produce, how to produce it, and who receives it, and choices involving scarce resources carry an opportunity cost as a result. In short, scarcity isn't a flaw in how economies are run, it's the underlying condition that makes economic decision-making necessary in the first place.
