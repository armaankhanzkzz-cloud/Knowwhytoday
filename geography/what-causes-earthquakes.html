What Causes Earthquakes?

Geography

Earthquakes occur when accumulated stress in Earth's crust is released suddenly, usually along faults. Most significant earthquakes are associated with the movement and interaction of tectonic plates.

Why are some earthquakes more destructive than others?

Damage depends on factors such as magnitude, depth, distance from populated areas, ground conditions, building quality, and the duration of shaking.

What is a fault, and why does stress build up there?

Earth's outer shell is broken into roughly fifteen major tectonic plates, huge slabs of rock that move very slowly relative to one another, typically just a few centimeters a year. Where these plates meet or grind against each other, cracks called faults form in the crust. Rather than sliding smoothly, the rock on either side of a fault tends to catch and stick due to friction, even while the surrounding plates continue their slow motion. This causes stress to build up gradually in the rock over months, years, or even centuries, in much the same way bending a stick stores energy right up until the point it snaps.

What is elastic rebound theory?

The most widely accepted explanation for how this stored stress turns into an earthquake is called elastic rebound theory, first proposed by geologist Harry Fielding Reid after studying the 1906 San Francisco earthquake. According to this theory, rock on either side of a locked fault bends and stores energy the way a compressed spring does, without actually breaking, for as long as friction holds the fault in place. Once the built-up stress finally exceeds the strength holding the fault together, the rock suddenly slips and snaps back toward its original shape, releasing all that stored energy at once. That sudden release is what we feel and measure as an earthquake.

What actually happens at the moment of an earthquake?

The point underground where the fault actually begins to slip is called the focus, or hypocenter, while the point on the surface directly above it is called the epicenter, the location usually reported in news coverage of an earthquake. The released energy radiates outward from the focus in the form of seismic waves. Faster-moving primary waves, or P waves, arrive first, followed by slower secondary waves, or S waves, and finally surface waves, which travel along the ground itself and are usually responsible for most of the shaking and damage people actually experience.

How do scientists measure earthquakes?

Earthquakes are measured using instruments called seismographs, which detect and record the ground motion caused by seismic waves. The size of an earthquake is typically reported using the moment magnitude scale, which has largely replaced the older Richter scale, and both are logarithmic, meaning each whole number increase represents roughly a tenfold increase in the amplitude of ground shaking and a much larger jump in the actual energy released. Magnitude describes the overall size of an earthquake at its source, while a separate measure called intensity describes how strongly the shaking was actually felt or how much damage it caused at a specific location, which can vary considerably depending on distance from the epicenter and local ground conditions.

Are all earthquakes caused by tectonic plates?

The large majority are, with most earthquakes directly tied to the movement and interaction of Earth's plates. Not all earthquakes fit this pattern, however. Volcanic earthquakes occur when moving magma fractures the surrounding rock beneath a volcano, and these can sometimes serve as an early warning sign of an approaching eruption. Human activity can trigger earthquakes too, including underground mining, large reservoirs, and certain industrial processes that inject fluid into the ground, though earthquakes caused this way are generally much smaller than major tectonic events.

Can earthquakes actually be predicted?

Not with any real precision, and it's worth being clear and honest about that rather than suggesting otherwise. Scientists can identify which regions are at higher long-term risk based on their location along fault lines and plate boundaries, and can estimate rough statistical patterns, such as the average interval between major earthquakes on a specific fault based on its past history. What scientists currently cannot do is reliably predict the exact time, location, and size of a specific future earthquake with any useful precision, and no scientifically validated method for doing so currently exists, despite ongoing research into the subject.

In short

Earthquakes happen when stress that has slowly built up along a locked fault is suddenly released, sending seismic waves through the ground as the rock snaps back into a less strained shape. The vast majority are tied to the slow, ongoing movement of Earth's tectonic plates, though volcanic activity and certain human activities can trigger earthquakes as well. While scientists understand the underlying mechanics well and can assess long-term regional risk, precisely predicting when and where a specific earthquake will strike remains beyond what current science can reliably do.
