What Causes Ocean Currents?

Geography

Ocean currents are driven by factors including wind, differences in temperature and salinity, Earth's rotation, and the shape of ocean basins. Surface currents and deep-water circulation transport heat and influence regional climates.

Why are ocean currents geographically important?

They redistribute heat around the planet and can influence rainfall, coastal temperatures, marine ecosystems, and weather patterns.

What sets surface currents moving in the first place?

The ocean's surface currents, generally the top few hundred meters of water, are driven mainly by wind. As wind blows steadily across the ocean surface, friction drags the topmost layer of water along with it. That moving layer then drags the water just beneath it, which in turn drags the layer below that, transferring motion downward through the water column, though each successive layer moves a little slower and in a slightly different direction than the one above it.

Why don't currents just flow in a straight line with the wind?

Because Earth is rotating, moving objects, including wind and water, don't travel in a perfectly straight line relative to the surface beneath them. This apparent deflection is called the Coriolis effect, and it bends currents to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. Combined with the wind patterns that drive them and the shape of the ocean basins that contain them, this deflection curves surface currents into enormous circular loops called gyres, which rotate clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counterclockwise in the Southern Hemisphere. Each major ocean basin has at least one dominant gyre organizing its surface circulation.

What is the Gulf Stream, and why is it so well known?

The Gulf Stream is one of the most famous of these currents, a powerful, fast-moving band of warm water that flows northward along the eastern coast of North America as part of the North Atlantic gyre. Despite being relatively narrow, generally under 100 kilometers wide, it's remarkably fast and deep, carrying an enormous volume of warm tropical water toward higher latitudes. As it travels, it releases heat into the atmosphere along its path, which is a major reason why parts of northwestern Europe enjoy notably milder winters than other regions at similar latitudes elsewhere in the world.

What drives currents deep below the surface?

Below the wind-driven layer, an entirely different mechanism takes over. Deep ocean currents, known collectively as thermohaline circulation, are driven by differences in water density, which depend on both temperature and salinity, or salt content. In certain polar regions, particularly near Greenland and Iceland, surface water becomes extremely cold, and as sea ice forms, it leaves extra salt behind in the surrounding water. This colder, saltier water becomes dense enough to sink all the way to the ocean floor, and as it sinks, surface water flows in to replace it, setting a slow-moving deep current into motion that can take roughly a thousand years to complete a single full loop around the planet's ocean basins, sometimes referred to as the global conveyor belt.

What is upwelling, and why does it matter for ocean life?

In some regions, wind patterns push surface water away from a coastline or away from the equator, and deeper water rises up to take its place, a process called upwelling. Because deep water tends to be rich in nutrients that have settled out of the surface layer over time, upwelling carries those nutrients back up toward the sunlit surface, where they can support large blooms of plankton and, in turn, some of the richest and most productive fishing grounds in the world.

In short

Ocean currents form through two very different mechanisms working at once. Near the surface, wind drags water into motion, and Earth's rotation curves that motion into vast circulating gyres. In the deep ocean, differences in temperature and salinity drive a much slower, density-based circulation that connects the world's ocean basins into a single, extremely slow-moving global system. Together, these wind-driven and density-driven currents move enormous amounts of heat, nutrients, and water around the planet, shaping regional climates and supporting ecosystems far beyond the coastlines they pass.
