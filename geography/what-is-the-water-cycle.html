What Is the Water Cycle?

Geography

The water cycle describes the continuous movement of water between the atmosphere, land, oceans, and living organisms. Major processes include evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, infiltration, runoff, and groundwater flow.

Why is the water cycle important?

It continually redistributes freshwater and helps regulate ecosystems, weather, climate, and the availability of water for human societies.

What actually gets the whole cycle moving?

The water cycle is powered by heat from the sun. Solar energy heats the surface of oceans, lakes, rivers, and soil, causing liquid water to evaporate, turning it into water vapor that rises into the atmosphere. Plants add to this process too, drawing water up through their roots and releasing water vapor into the air through tiny pores in their leaves, a process called transpiration. Because evaporation from land and water surfaces and transpiration from plants are so closely linked, scientists often measure them together as a single combined process called evapotranspiration.

What happens once water vapor is in the atmosphere?

As water vapor rises higher into the atmosphere, it cools, and cooler air can hold less water vapor than warmer air. This causes the vapor to condense back into tiny liquid droplets or ice crystals, clustering together to form clouds. Once these droplets grow large and heavy enough, they fall back to Earth's surface as precipitation, whether as rain, snow, sleet, or hail, depending on the temperature conditions they fall through.

What happens to water once it reaches the ground?

Precipitation that lands on the ground can take several different paths from there. Some of it soaks into the soil through a process called infiltration, gradually working its way down until it reaches an underground layer of water-saturated rock or sediment called an aquifer, where it's stored as groundwater, sometimes for a very long time. Water that doesn't infiltrate instead becomes surface runoff, flowing over the land into streams, rivers, and eventually lakes or oceans. In colder regions, precipitation may instead fall as snow and accumulate for months before melting and entering the cycle later as snowmelt.

Does water spend the same amount of time in every part of the cycle?

Not even close. Water moves through different parts of the cycle at dramatically different speeds, a concept scientists call residence time, the average amount of time a water molecule spends in a given reservoir before moving on. Water typically stays in the atmosphere for only about ten days before falling as precipitation, while water sitting in the ocean can remain there for roughly 3,000 years on average before evaporating again. Groundwater can have an even wider range of residence times, with some ancient underground reservoirs, sometimes called fossil water, holding onto water for thousands of years before it ever reemerges.

Is the water cycle a closed loop with a fixed starting point?

Not really, it's better understood as a continuous cycle with no true beginning or end, since the total amount of water on Earth stays roughly constant even as it's constantly rearranged between the atmosphere, oceans, land, ice, and living things. Water can be described as beginning at any point in the cycle, evaporating from the ocean, falling as mountain snow, soaking into an aquifer, or being released by a plant through transpiration, and the description still holds, since the water simply continues cycling onward from wherever the story happens to start.

In short

The water cycle is powered by the sun's heat, which drives evaporation and transpiration, sending water vapor into the atmosphere where it cools, condenses into clouds, and eventually returns to the surface as precipitation. From there, water either soaks into the ground to become groundwater or flows across the surface as runoff, before eventually making its way back toward the ocean to begin the process again. Different parts of this cycle move at wildly different speeds, from an atmospheric passage lasting just days to groundwater that can remain hidden underground for thousands of years, all part of the same continuous, planet-spanning system.
