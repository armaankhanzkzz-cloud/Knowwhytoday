Why Do Deserts Receive So Little Rainfall?

Geography

Many deserts occur in regions where atmospheric circulation suppresses rainfall, such as subtropical high-pressure belts. Other deserts form in rain shadows, along cold ocean currents, or far from major sources of moisture.

Are all deserts hot?

No. A desert is defined primarily by aridity rather than temperature. Some deserts are extremely cold, including polar deserts.

Why do so many deserts sit at similar latitudes around the world?

Many of the world's largest deserts, including the Sahara, the Arabian Desert, and the Australian Outback, sit in belts roughly 15 to 30 degrees north and south of the equator, and this isn't a coincidence. Near the equator, intense heating causes air to rise, cool, and release much of its moisture as heavy tropical rainfall. That same air, now much drier, continues moving toward the poles at high altitude until it collides with other circulating air and sinks back down to the surface around those subtropical latitudes. As this air sinks, it compresses and warms again, which lowers its relative humidity even further and actively suppresses cloud formation, creating the persistent high-pressure, low-rainfall conditions found in these desert belts, sometimes called the horse latitudes.

Why do mountains create deserts on one side but not the other?

Mountain ranges can produce a very localized but powerful desert-forming effect known as a rain shadow. When moist air is forced to rise over a mountain range, it cools as it climbs, and that cooling causes much of its moisture to condense and fall as rain or snow on the windward side, the side facing the incoming air. By the time the air crosses the peak and descends the other side, it has lost most of its moisture and warms back up as it drops in elevation, arriving at the surface both dry and hot. This is why a lush, heavily forested mountain slope can sit directly beside a comparatively barren desert on the opposite side of the very same range, as seen with deserts like the Gobi in the shadow of the Himalayas or Death Valley in the shadow of the Sierra Nevada.

Why are some coastal deserts dry despite sitting right next to the ocean?

It seems counterintuitive, but some of the driest places on Earth sit directly along a coastline. Along certain western coastlines, cold, deep ocean water rises up to the surface in a process called upwelling, and this cold water cools the air sitting just above it. Cooler air holds less moisture and resists the kind of rising, convective motion that normally builds rain clouds, so relatively little precipitation ever forms, even with a vast ocean sitting right there. This is the primary reason the Atacama Desert in Chile and the Namib Desert in Namibia, both hugging a coastline, count among the driest places on the planet.

Why does distance from the ocean matter for a desert's dryness?

A factor sometimes called continentality also plays a role for deserts sitting deep within the interior of a large continent, far from any ocean. Since virtually all atmospheric moisture ultimately originates from evaporation off open water, air masses traveling long distances over land gradually lose much of the moisture they started with before ever reaching these interior regions, leaving continental interiors chronically starved of rainfall regardless of latitude or nearby mountain ranges.

How can Antarctica, covered in ice, actually count as a desert?

This sounds contradictory at first, but it comes down to how a desert is actually defined, by how little precipitation falls, not by how much ice or snow happens to be sitting on the ground already. Extremely cold air simply can't hold much moisture at all, which severely limits how much snow can fall in polar regions in the first place. Antarctica's interior receives only a small amount of precipitation each year, technically qualifying much of the continent as desert, even though the ice that has slowly accumulated there over millions of years makes it look like the opposite of a dry place at first glance.

In short

Deserts form through several distinct mechanisms that all end in the same basic result: persistently low rainfall. Large-scale atmospheric circulation creates dry, sinking air around the subtropics, mountain ranges strip moisture out of air before it ever reaches their leeward side, cold ocean currents suppress rainfall along certain coastlines, and sheer distance from open water starves other regions of moisture altogether. Even the icy poles qualify as deserts under this same basic definition, since what makes a desert a desert is how little precipitation falls, not how hot, cold, sandy, or icy the landscape happens to look.
