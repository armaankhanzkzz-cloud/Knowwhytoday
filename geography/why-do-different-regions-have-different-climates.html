Why Do Different Regions Have Different Climates?

Geography

Climate varies because different places receive different amounts of solar energy and are influenced by latitude, altitude, atmospheric circulation, distance from oceans, ocean currents, and topography.

How does latitude affect climate?

Places near the equator generally receive more direct solar radiation throughout the year, while higher latitudes receive sunlight at lower angles and experience greater seasonal variation.

How does altitude change the climate of a place?

Elevation matters just as much as latitude in shaping climate, and it works in a fairly predictable way. Air temperature drops steadily as altitude increases, at an average rate of roughly 6.5 degrees Celsius for every kilometer of elevation gained, a relationship called the environmental lapse rate. This is why a mountain can have snow-capped peaks even in a region that's hot and tropical at sea level, and why cities at higher elevation tend to have noticeably cooler average temperatures than nearby cities at lower elevation, even at the exact same latitude. Mountain ranges add a second effect on top of this cooling, since they can physically block air masses from passing through, separating a coastal region with one type of climate from an inland region with an entirely different one just on the other side of the range.

Why do coastal regions and inland regions have such different temperature patterns?

Being near an ocean tends to moderate temperatures throughout the year, producing what's called a maritime climate, with relatively mild winters and relatively cool summers compared with places at a similar latitude further inland. This happens because water heats up and cools down far more slowly than land does, so nearby air temperatures stay more stable across the seasons. Deep inland, far from any large body of water, regions instead experience what's called a continental climate, marked by much larger swings between summer and winter temperatures, since the land beneath that air heats up and cools down quickly with the seasons rather than staying buffered by a nearby ocean.

How do broader wind and pressure patterns fit into all of this?

Underlying many regional climate differences is the same large-scale pattern of rising and sinking air that circles the globe in bands, driven ultimately by how unevenly the sun heats different latitudes. Consistently rising air near the equator tends to produce heavy, regular rainfall and lush vegetation, while consistently sinking air around the subtropics tends to suppress rainfall and produce some of the world's driest regions, a mechanism covered in more detail in our article on why deserts form. These same global wind and pressure patterns also help set up the seasonal wind reversals behind monsoon climates and help drive the ocean currents that redistribute heat around the planet, both of which are explored further in our articles on monsoons and ocean currents.

So how do all of these factors combine to shape a specific place's climate?

No single factor determines a region's climate on its own, it's really the combined effect of several factors acting together at once. Latitude sets the basic amount of solar energy a region receives, altitude and mountain ranges modify temperature and can block or redirect moisture, distance from the ocean determines how stable or extreme seasonal temperatures tend to be, and large-scale atmospheric and ocean circulation patterns determine how much rainfall, and what kind of prevailing winds, a region typically experiences. Two places at the exact same latitude can end up with dramatically different climates depending on how these other factors happen to combine in each location, which is why climate maps show such a rich, varied patchwork rather than simple, uniform bands running neatly around the globe.

In short

Regional climate differences come from several overlapping factors working together rather than any single cause. Latitude determines how directly sunlight strikes a region, altitude cools the air as elevation increases and mountains can divide neighboring climates sharply, and proximity to the ocean determines how much seasonal temperatures swing between summer and winter. Layered on top of all this, large-scale patterns of atmospheric circulation and ocean currents redistribute heat and moisture around the planet, shaping everything from persistent desert belts to monsoon seasons to the relatively narrow, coast-hugging bands of ocean-moderated climate found in many parts of the world.
