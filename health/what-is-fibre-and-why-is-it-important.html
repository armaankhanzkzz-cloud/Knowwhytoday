What Is Fibre and Why Is It Important?

Health

Dietary fibre is found mainly in plant foods and supports normal bowel function. It can also contribute to satiety and healthy blood glucose and cholesterol levels.

Which foods contain fibre?

Whole grains, beans, lentils, vegetables, fruits, nuts, and seeds are important sources of dietary fibre.

What is dietary fibre, at a basic level?

Dietary fibre refers to carbohydrates found in plant foods that the human digestive system can't break down using its own enzymes. Unlike sugars and starches, which are digested and absorbed in the small intestine, fibre passes through largely intact until it reaches the colon, where it plays a very different role than most other things we eat.

What's the difference between soluble and insoluble fibre?

Fibre is generally grouped into two broad types based on how it behaves in water. Soluble fibre dissolves and forms a gel-like substance in the digestive tract, and most types are readily broken down by bacteria in the colon. Insoluble fibre doesn't dissolve, and instead adds bulk to stool and helps move material through the digestive tract more efficiently, which is why it's particularly associated with preventing constipation and supporting regular bowel movements. Most plant foods contain a mix of both types rather than purely one or the other, which is part of why eating a variety of whole plant foods tends to be recommended over relying on a single source.

What happens to fibre once it reaches the colon?

Much of the soluble fibre that survives digestion becomes food for the trillions of bacteria living in the colon, through a process called fermentation. These gut bacteria break fibre down into compounds called short-chain fatty acids, including butyrate, propionate, and acetate, which serve as an important energy source for the cells lining the colon and appear to support a healthy balance of gut bacteria more broadly. This is part of why fibre is sometimes described as feeding the gut microbiome rather than feeding the person directly, since much of its benefit in the colon comes from what bacteria do with it rather than from fibre itself being absorbed.

How does fibre affect blood sugar and cholesterol?

Soluble fibre's gel-forming property does more than just support digestion. By forming a thick gel in the digestive tract, it can slow down how quickly sugar from food is absorbed into the bloodstream, which helps prevent the sharp spikes in blood glucose that can follow a meal. Soluble fibre can also bind to substances called bile acids, which the body needs cholesterol to produce, and carry them out through digestion, prompting the body to draw more cholesterol out of the bloodstream to replace them. These two mechanisms are the main reasons dietary fibre is often linked to healthier blood sugar control and lower cholesterol levels over time.

How much fibre do people generally need?

Recommended daily fibre intake varies somewhat by age and sex, but general guidelines commonly cited by health organizations suggest adult women under 50 aim for around 25 grams a day and adult men under 50 aim for around 38 grams, with slightly lower targets, around 21 and 30 grams respectively, for adults over 50. Despite this, surveys have repeatedly found that most adults fall well short of these targets, often consuming closer to 15 grams a day. Because a sudden large increase in fibre intake can cause temporary digestive discomfort such as gas or bloating, health guidance generally recommends increasing fibre intake gradually and drinking enough fluids alongside it, rather than making a dramatic change all at once. As with any significant dietary change, anyone with existing digestive conditions or other health concerns should check with a healthcare provider about what's appropriate for them specifically.

In short

Dietary fibre is the part of plant foods the body can't fully digest on its own, and it does much of its work precisely because of that. Insoluble fibre adds bulk and supports regular digestion, while soluble fibre forms a gel that helps steady blood sugar and cholesterol, and also feeds the beneficial bacteria living in the colon through fermentation. Most people fall short of recommended daily amounts, which is part of why whole grains, legumes, fruits, and vegetables are consistently highlighted as valuable, everyday parts of a balanced diet.
