What Is the Role of Protein in the Body?

Health

Protein supplies amino acids used to build and repair tissues and to produce enzymes, hormones, antibodies, and other important molecules.

What is protein actually made of?

Every protein is built from smaller units called amino acids, strung together in a chain and then folded into a specific three dimensional shape. There are twenty different amino acids that the human body uses to build its proteins, and the particular order they're strung in determines what the finished protein looks like and what job it can do. Nine of these are called essential amino acids, which simply means the body cannot make them on its own and has to get them from food. The rest are considered nonessential, since the body is able to build them itself from other raw materials, although a few of them become important to get from food too during illness or periods of stress.

Where can protein come from?

Protein is found in foods such as eggs, dairy products, fish, meat, legumes, nuts, seeds, and other plant foods. Foods that supply all nine essential amino acids in reasonable amounts are called complete proteins, and most animal foods fall into this category. Many plant foods are considered incomplete proteins because they run low in one or more essential amino acid, though this is rarely a concern in practice, since eating a variety of plant foods across the day, such as rice with beans or hummus with pita, supplies the full set the body needs.

How does the body turn food protein into something it can use?

Digestion breaks whole proteins back down into their individual amino acids so the body can absorb them. Chewing starts the physical breakdown, and once food reaches the stomach, acid and an enzyme called pepsin begin unfolding the protein and cutting it into shorter fragments. Most of this work finishes in the small intestine, where enzymes released by the pancreas, along with enzymes on the intestinal lining itself, chop those fragments down into single amino acids and very short chains. These are then absorbed through the intestinal wall into the bloodstream and carried to the liver, which helps distribute them to wherever in the body they're needed. From there, cells use the amino acids as a kind of raw material, reassembling them into whichever new proteins that particular tissue requires.

What jobs does protein do inside the body?

Protein's roles reach into nearly every part of how the body works. Structural proteins like collagen and keratin form the framework of skin, hair, bone, and connective tissue, while proteins such as actin and myosin let muscles contract. Many proteins act as enzymes, speeding up the chemical reactions that keep metabolism running, and others work as hormones, such as insulin, that carry signals between organs. Antibodies, which are also proteins, help the immune system recognize and respond to harmful invaders. Still other proteins act as transporters, such as hemoglobin, which carries oxygen through the blood, or help regulate the body's fluid balance and its acid base balance. If the body doesn't have enough carbohydrate or fat available, it can even break protein down and use it as a backup source of energy, though that isn't its main purpose.

How much protein does a person need?

Protein needs vary with a person's age, sex, body size, and activity level, and needs also go up during pregnancy and breastfeeding. A commonly cited baseline for a generally healthy, sedentary adult is around 0.8 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day, though many nutrition scientists and updated dietary guidance suggest that somewhat higher amounts can be more supportive of muscle health, especially for older adults or people who exercise regularly. Unlike fat or carbohydrate, the body has no dedicated way to store extra protein for later, so a steady supply from food across the day, rather than one large amount at a single meal, helps the body keep making the proteins it needs.

What happens if the body doesn't get enough protein?

Because protein is involved in building and repairing so many tissues, a diet that consistently falls short on protein can eventually show up as slower wound healing, loss of muscle mass, weaker immune function, and, in more severe or prolonged cases, swelling and other complications. In parts of the world with limited access to food, inadequate protein intake remains a serious public health concern, particularly for children. In wealthier countries, outright protein deficiency is uncommon among people who eat a varied diet with enough total calories, though certain medical conditions, restrictive diets, or reduced appetite can still put someone at risk. Anyone concerned about whether they're getting enough protein is best served by speaking with a doctor or registered dietitian, who can look at their individual health picture.

In short

Protein is broken down into amino acids during digestion and then rebuilt inside the body into whatever new proteins a given tissue needs. Those proteins go on to form the structure of muscle, skin, and bone, drive the chemical reactions of metabolism as enzymes, carry signals as hormones, defend the body as antibodies, and transport substances like oxygen through the blood. Because the body can't store protein the way it stores fat or carbohydrate, a steady supply from a varied diet helps keep all of these functions running smoothly.
