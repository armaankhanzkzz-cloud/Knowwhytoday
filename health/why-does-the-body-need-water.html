Why Does the Body Need Water?

Health

Water is essential for regulating body temperature, transporting substances, supporting chemical reactions, and maintaining normal cellular function.

How much of the body is actually water?

Water makes up roughly 50 to 65 percent of an adult's body weight, though the exact share depends on things like age, sex, and how much muscle versus fat tissue a person has, since muscle holds more water than fat does. It's found inside every cell, in the blood and other body fluids, and even within bone. Because water is such a large part of the body's makeup to begin with, even a relatively small loss of it can be enough to affect how well the body works.

What roles does water actually play in the body?

Water's job in the body can be thought of in a few broad categories. It acts as a transport medium, since blood, which is mostly water, carries oxygen, nutrients, and hormones to cells and carries waste products away. It serves as the setting for countless chemical reactions, dissolving substances so the body's enzymes and other processes can do their work. It cushions and lubricates tissues, including the joints and the tissue surrounding the brain and spinal cord. And it plays a central role in regulating body temperature, since the body cools itself by releasing heat through the skin as sweat evaporates.

What happens during dehydration?

Dehydration happens when the body loses more fluid than it takes in, whether from not drinking enough, sweating heavily, or losing fluids through illness such as vomiting or diarrhea. Early signs in adults typically include thirst, a dry mouth, fatigue, headache, dizziness, and urinating less often than usual, with urine appearing darker in color. Mild dehydration can usually be corrected by drinking more fluids, but as fluid loss becomes more severe, symptoms can progress to a rapid heartbeat, confusion, fainting, or an inability to keep fluids down, at which point it becomes a medical emergency that needs prompt treatment. Older adults and young children are at higher risk, since older adults often lose some of their sense of thirst as they age, and children can lose fluids quickly through vomiting or diarrhea relative to their smaller body size.

How much water does the body actually need each day?

There's no single number that fits everyone, since fluid needs shift with body size, activity level, climate, and overall health. A commonly cited general guideline suggests around 11.5 cups of total fluids a day for women and about 15.5 cups for men, and this figure already includes fluid from food as well as drinks, since fruits, vegetables, soups, and other foods all contribute water too. The familiar advice to drink eight glasses of water a day is a reasonable and easy to remember target for many people, even though it isn't based on one specific study, and thirst itself remains one of the simplest everyday guides to drinking enough.

Can drinking too much water actually be a problem?

For most people going about ordinary daily life, drinking too much water is genuinely rare, since healthy kidneys are good at getting rid of excess fluid. The risk becomes more real in specific situations, such as endurance events where athletes drink far more fluid than they're losing through sweat, which can dilute the sodium in the blood and lead to a condition called hyponatremia. Mild hyponatremia can cause nausea, headache, and confusion, and severe cases are a genuine medical emergency. This isn't a reason for most people to worry about their everyday water intake, but it's part of why guidance for endurance exercise generally focuses on drinking to match fluid losses rather than drinking as much as possible.

In short

Water makes up more than half of the body's weight and touches nearly every system it has, from transporting nutrients and oxygen through the blood to cushioning tissues and cooling the body through sweat. Because the body is constantly losing water through breathing, sweating, and going to the bathroom, it needs a steady supply back, and needs shift with activity, climate, and individual health. Thirst and urine color remain simple everyday guides, and while dehydration is the far more common risk, drinking sensibly rather than excessively, especially during intense or prolonged exercise, matters too.
