Why Is Handwashing Important?

Health

Handwashing with soap and water removes microorganisms and contaminants from the hands and helps reduce the spread of infectious diseases.

How do germs end up on our hands in the first place?

A surprising amount of illness traces back to contact with feces, whether from people or animals. A single gram of human feces, about the weight of a paper clip, can contain a trillion germs, and these can end up on hands after using the toilet, changing a diaper, or even handling raw meat that carries traces most people never see. Hands also pick up germs from coughs, sneezes, and everyday contact with surfaces like doorknobs and handrails that someone else has touched. Once germs are on the hands, they spread easily, since people touch their eyes, nose, and mouth far more often than they realize, and they can also transfer germs directly onto food, drinks, or shared objects.

When should hands be washed?

Important times include before eating or preparing food, after using the toilet, after coughing or sneezing, and after contact with potentially contaminated surfaces or materials. Other key moments include after touching garbage, after caring for someone who is sick, after handling pet food or animal waste, and after blowing your nose. Building these moments into a routine matters more than any single wash, since it's the cumulative effect of consistently interrupting germ transfer that keeps illness from spreading.

Why is soap more effective than water alone?

Washing with just water does remove some germs, but soap does a noticeably better job. Soap contains surfactants, compounds that help lift oil, dirt, and microbes off the surface of the skin so they can be rinsed away, and using soap also tends to make people scrub more thoroughly than they would with water alone. The rubbing and lathering itself creates friction that physically dislodges germs, particularly from areas like the fingernails where they tend to concentrate. Plain soap has been found to work just as well as soap marketed as antibacterial, so there's no added benefit to reaching for one over the other for everyday handwashing.

What's the correct technique?

Health authorities recommend a simple five step process: wet the hands with clean, running water, apply soap, and lather thoroughly, making sure to cover the backs of the hands, between the fingers, and under the nails. Scrub for at least 20 seconds, about the time it takes to hum a short song through twice, then rinse well under clean running water and dry with a clean towel or by air drying. Water temperature doesn't meaningfully affect how many germs are removed, so cold or warm water both work, and warmer water may cause more skin irritation with frequent washing. When soap and water aren't available, an alcohol based hand sanitizer with at least 60 percent alcohol is a reasonable substitute, though it doesn't work as well against every kind of germ and isn't a full replacement for washing when hands are visibly dirty.

How much difference does handwashing actually make?

The evidence is substantial. Community handwashing education has been found to reduce diarrheal illness by around a quarter to two fifths, and by more than half among people with weakened immune systems, while also cutting respiratory illnesses like colds by around a fifth. In schools, handwashing programs have reduced absences linked to stomach illness by close to a third to over half. The effect is even larger in parts of the world with limited access to clean water and soap, where diarrheal diseases and pneumonia have historically accounted for a substantial share of deaths among children under five, particularly in settings with limited access to clean water, sanitation, and healthcare, two of the leading causes of death in young children, and where handwashing with soap could prevent illness in roughly a third of diarrhea cases and a fifth of respiratory infections in that age group.

In short

Hands pick up germs constantly, often from sources people don't think about, like their own feces or a doorknob touched by someone else. Soap works better than water alone because it lifts germs from the skin and encourages more thorough scrubbing, and following the five step wash, lather, scrub, rinse, dry routine for at least 20 seconds removes far more of them than a quick rinse. The payoff shows up in the numbers, with community and school based handwashing programs consistently linked to meaningfully less diarrheal and respiratory illness, particularly for young children.
