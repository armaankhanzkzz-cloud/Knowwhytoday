Why Is Regular Physical Activity Important?

Health

Regular physical activity supports cardiovascular fitness, muscular strength, bone health, mobility, and overall well-being.

What actually happens in the body during exercise?

Physical activity is any movement that engages the skeletal muscles and raises the body's energy use above its resting level. During aerobic activity, the heart beats faster and breathing quickens to deliver more oxygen to working muscles, and over time this repeated demand makes the heart and lungs more efficient. Muscles that are regularly worked against resistance respond by growing stronger, and bones respond to the mechanical stress of weight bearing activity by maintaining or building density. These physical adaptations are part of why the benefits of activity extend well beyond the time spent actually moving.

How much activity is useful?

The appropriate amount varies by age and individual circumstances, but consistent movement and regular exercise are generally more beneficial than prolonged inactivity. For most adults, a common target is at least 150 minutes a week of moderate intensity aerobic activity, such as brisk walking, or 75 minutes of vigorous intensity activity, such as jogging, along with muscle strengthening activity on two or more days a week. This doesn't need to happen in one long session, since the total can be split across the week, for example into 30 minutes a day, five days a week. Even amounts below this target still provide some benefit, and any movement is generally considered better than none.

What does regular activity do for the heart and long term disease risk?

Regular physical activity is strongly linked to a lower risk of heart disease, stroke, high blood pressure, and type 2 diabetes. It's also associated with a reduced risk of several common cancers and can help people already managing a chronic condition control their symptoms and improve daily functioning. Part of this comes from physical activity's effect on body weight, since it increases how many calories the body uses, but the benefits to the heart and blood vessels appear even independent of any change in weight.

Does exercise affect the brain and mental health too?

Yes, and this is one of the more actively researched areas of exercise science. Regular physical activity is linked to improved mood, reduced symptoms of anxiety and depression, and better sleep, and even a single session of moderate to vigorous activity can produce short term improvements in memory and thinking skills. Over the longer term, staying physically active is associated with a reduced risk of cognitive decline and dementia, including Alzheimer's disease, although researchers are still working out the exact biological mechanisms behind this connection.

What happens to the body with long term inactivity?

Prolonged inactivity works against nearly everything regular movement supports. Muscles that go unused gradually lose strength and mass, bones can lose density over time, and the cardiovascular system becomes less efficient, all of which make everyday tasks and independent living harder, particularly as people age. Inactivity is also linked to a higher risk of the same chronic diseases that physical activity helps guard against. This is one of the reasons health guidance frames the comparison not as exercise versus rest, but as movement versus prolonged sitting, since reducing the amount of time spent inactive brings benefits on its own, separate from any structured workout.

In short

Physical activity strengthens the heart, lungs, muscles, and bones through the repeated, gradual demands it places on them, and those adaptations translate into a lower risk of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and several cancers. Its reach extends to the brain as well, supporting mood, sleep, and long term cognitive health. While specific targets like 150 minutes a week offer a useful benchmark, the underlying principle is simpler: consistent movement, in whatever amount fits a person's life and ability, generally serves the body better than staying still.
