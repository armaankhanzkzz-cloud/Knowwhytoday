What Was the Silk Road?

History

The Silk Road was a network of land and maritime trade routes connecting parts of East Asia, Central Asia, the Middle East, Africa, and Europe.

How did the Silk Road actually get started?

Its origins trace back to the Han Dynasty in China, around the 2nd century BCE. In 138 BCE, Emperor Wu sent an official named Zhang Qian west on a diplomatic mission, hoping to build an alliance against a rival nomadic group. Zhang Qian didn't succeed at that original goal. Along the way, though, he learned about wealthy kingdoms and established trade networks further west than China had previously had close contact with. His reports back to the Han court opened the door to regular trade and diplomatic contact, and the routes grew steadily from there.

What travelled along these routes?

Merchants exchanged goods such as silk, spices, textiles, metals, and ceramics, while ideas, technologies, religions, and cultural practices also spread. Silk was the good that gave the route its name, and Chinese silk was highly prized in the Roman world and beyond. But the exchange went both directions. The Han imported horses, glassware, and precious metals from Central Asia, Persia, and Rome. Buddhism spread from India into Central Asia and China largely along these same paths, and later, Islam spread west to east along parts of the network as well. Along with goods and beliefs, less welcome things travelled too, including diseases.

Was it really just one road?

No. The name suggests a single path, but the Silk Road was actually a shifting web of many routes, both overland and maritime. Overland routes ran from the Chinese city of Chang'an through the Gansu Corridor, split around the harsh Taklamakan Desert, and rejoined further west before continuing through Central Asia, Persia, and on to the Mediterranean. Maritime routes ran alongside these, connecting Chinese ports to Southeast Asia, India, and eventually the Middle East by sea. Which routes were busiest shifted constantly, depending on which empires controlled which territory and how safe travel was at any given time.

When was the Silk Road at its busiest?

Trade along the routes grew steadily through the Han and Tang dynasties in China, but the single biggest boost came from the Mongol Empire in the 13th and 14th centuries. The Mongol conquests brought much of the overland route under Mongol control, creating a period of relative political stability that made long-distance travel easier. This period became known as the Pax Mongolica, and the relative safety it created let merchants and travelers move across Eurasia more easily than before. It was during this window that the Venetian merchant Marco Polo made his famous journey to the court of the Mongol ruler Kublai Khan, and his written account became one of medieval Europe's most famous sources of information about Asia.

Why did the Silk Road eventually decline?

A few things happened close together. The Mongol Empire broke apart after the mid-14th century, and the political stability that had protected long-distance travel went with it. Around the same time, the Black Death, which likely spread west along these same trade routes, devastated major trading cities and disrupted the merchants and infrastructure the routes depended on. The final blow came amid major changes in the political and commercial landscape of the routes. The Ottoman Empire took control of Constantinople in 1453, while European sailors increasingly developed sea routes to Asia that offered alternatives to long overland journeys, further reducing the overland Silk Road's importance.

Where does the name "Silk Road" actually come from?

Interestingly, nobody who actually traveled these routes called them the Silk Road. The name is a much later invention, coined by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen in 1877, more than a thousand years after the trade network first took shape. He used the term to describe the historical trade connections between Han Dynasty China and the Roman world. It caught on as a convenient label for what had always really been a much larger and messier collection of routes, rather than a name anyone living through that history would have recognized.

In short

The Silk Road developed from expanding networks of trade and diplomacy linking China with Central Asia and regions farther west, especially from the 2nd century BCE onward, and grew into a sprawling network of overland and maritime routes linking East Asia to the Mediterranean world. Goods like silk moved alongside religions, technologies, and eventually disease, connecting societies that had previously known little about each other. Trade peaked under the relative safety of the Mongol Empire before political fragmentation, plague, and the rise of maritime trade routes wound it down by the 15th century. Even the name is a later invention, coined centuries after the network it describes had already reshaped much of Eurasia.
