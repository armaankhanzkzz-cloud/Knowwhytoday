Why Was the Roman Empire Important?

History

Rome influenced law, government, engineering, language, military organisation, and urban development across a vast territory.

What helped Rome govern such a large empire?

Roads, administrative institutions, military organisation, taxation, and communication networks helped connect and manage its territories.

What did Roman law leave behind?

One of Rome's most enduring legacies is its legal system. Early Roman law was codified around 451 BCE in a set of rules known as the Twelve Tables, covering property, family matters, and contracts in a structured way. Centuries later, Emperor Justinian ordered a massive compilation of Roman law known as the Corpus Juris Civilis in the sixth century CE, which organized and preserved centuries of Roman legal thinking. This body of law went on to become a foundational reference for the civil law systems used across much of continental Europe, Latin America, and other parts of the world today, making Roman legal concepts, such as contracts and property rights, still directly traceable in modern courtrooms.

How did Roman engineering change the ancient world, and still matters today?

The Romans were extraordinary builders. At its height, the empire maintained a road network stretching more than 50,000 miles, built with layered stone, sand, and gravel using techniques that set the standard for road building for the next 2,000 years, and parts of modern European highways still follow the same routes laid down by Roman engineers. Roman aqueducts carried fresh water across long distances using nothing but gravity and precisely calculated inclines, some stretching more than 60 miles to supply Rome's homes, baths, and public fountains. The Romans also developed a form of concrete stronger and more durable than much of what's used today, which allowed them to build structures like the Pantheon's dome, still standing after roughly 1,900 years as the largest unreinforced concrete dome ever constructed.

How did Latin shape the languages people speak today?

Latin, the administrative and everyday language of the Roman Empire, gradually evolved into the Romance languages, including Italian, French, Spanish, Portuguese, and Romanian, spoken natively by hundreds of millions of people today. Latin also survives directly in scientific naming systems, many legal terms, and religious traditions, and its influence can be felt in the vocabulary of English as well, even though English itself descends from a different language family.

How did citizenship help hold such a diverse empire together?

Rome didn't rule its vast territory purely through military force. Citizenship, along with Roman law and infrastructure such as roads and aqueducts, helped integrate the many different peoples the empire absorbed as it expanded. Citizenship was extended gradually and selectively to conquered populations over time, giving people across a huge and culturally diverse territory a genuine stake in the empire's stability and success, which helped foster loyalty in a way that pure military occupation alone couldn't have achieved.

Was Rome's success built only on positive achievements?

No, and it's worth being honest about that alongside everything Rome built. The empire's vast territory was assembled through centuries of military conquest, and the economy that supported its cities, buildings, and lifestyle relied heavily on slave labor, with enslaved people making up a substantial portion of the population across the empire at various points in its history. Roman expansion brought genuine hardship, violence, and the loss of independence for the many peoples conquered and absorbed into the empire. The lasting achievements in law, engineering, and governance that Rome is remembered for today existed alongside, and were often made possible by, this less admirable side of how the empire was built and sustained.

In short

Rome's importance comes from just how much of the modern world still carries its imprint, in legal systems built on Roman law, roads and water systems that pioneered engineering techniques still recognizable today, languages descended directly from Latin, and ideas about citizenship that helped hold together one of history's largest and most diverse empires. That legacy sits alongside a harder truth: Rome's power and prosperity were built through conquest and sustained in significant part by slavery, and a full account of why the empire mattered has to hold both sides of that story together.
