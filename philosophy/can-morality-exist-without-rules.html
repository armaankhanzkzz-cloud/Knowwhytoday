Can Morality Exist Without Rules?

Philosophy

Some ethical theories emphasise rules, while others focus on consequences, character, care, or context.

Why do ethical theories disagree?

They prioritise different principles and may reach different conclusions when values such as freedom, welfare, justice, and responsibility conflict.

What does a "rule-based" approach to morality look like?

The clearest rule-based approach comes from deontological ethics, most associated with Immanuel Kant, which holds that certain moral rules apply universally, regardless of the specific situation or its consequences. On this view, a rule against lying, for instance, holds in essentially every case, because the rule itself is what makes an action right or wrong, not the particular circumstances surrounding it. This gives clear, consistent guidance, but it's exactly this rigidity that some philosophers have pushed back against, arguing that real moral life is too varied and context-dependent to be captured by any fixed set of general rules.

What is moral particularism?

Philosopher Jonathan Dancy developed one of the most direct challenges to rule-based ethics, a position called moral particularism. Particularism holds that moral judgment doesn't depend on having a supply of general principles to apply, because the same feature of a situation, such as causing someone pain, can count as a reason against an action in one context and be morally irrelevant, or even count in its favor, in another, depending on the surrounding details. On this view, good moral judgment is less like applying a formula and more like a kind of perceptual sensitivity to what actually matters in a specific situation, developed through experience rather than derived from a rulebook. Critics worry that without any general principles at all, moral judgments risk becoming arbitrary or impossible to explain consistently to others.

What is the ethics of care?

A different approach that questions the centrality of abstract rules, known as the ethics of care, developed prominently in the 1980s through the work of thinkers such as psychologist Carol Gilligan and philosopher Nel Noddings, partly in response to what they saw as an overemphasis on abstract rights and rules in traditional ethical theory. Care ethics treats caring relationships, and close attention to the specific needs of the particular people involved, as the foundation of morality, rather than any impartial rule applied equally to everyone. Instead of asking "what rule covers this case?", a care ethicist tends to ask "what does this specific relationship, and this specific person's needs, actually call for right now?" This approach has been especially influential in fields like nursing, education, and social work, where relationships and context often matter enormously to what the right action actually is.

Can virtuous character guide action without explicit rules?

Virtue ethics offers another way of explaining how moral judgment can work without making explicit rules the central guide. Rather than consulting a rule each time, it holds that a person with well-developed character traits—honesty, courage, and fairness—can generally perceive and respond to a situation appropriately without consciously working through a formal rule. Aristotle described this practical skill through the idea of practical wisdom, developed through experience and habituation, similar to how a skilled craftsperson develops a feel for their work that cannot be fully written down as a set of instructions. Rules are not necessarily rejected outright; they are secondary to the deeper capacity for good judgment that enables a person to respond well to particular circumstances.

What's the argument for keeping rules around?

Defenders of rule-based ethics argue that abandoning general principles altogether comes with real costs. Rules offer predictability, letting people know in advance what's expected of them and what to expect from others. They also guard against bias and self-interest, since a person relying purely on in-the-moment judgment might unconsciously favor conclusions that happen to be convenient for them, whereas a fixed rule applies the same way regardless of who benefits. Rules can also make moral reasoning easier to explain and defend to other people, since pointing to a shared principle is often more persuasive than asserting a purely personal, situational judgment.

In short

Morality doesn't strictly require a fixed rulebook. Approaches like moral particularism, the ethics of care, and virtue ethics all offer accounts of how people can act well by attending closely to context, relationships, and character rather than applying general principles. At the same time, rules offer real practical benefits, predictability, consistency, and protection against bias, that purely situational judgment can struggle to match. In practice, moral thinking can draw on both general principles and context-sensitive judgment rather than relying purely on one or the other.
