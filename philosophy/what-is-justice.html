What Is Justice?

Philosophy

Justice concerns how benefits, burdens, rights, opportunities, and responsibilities should be distributed or recognised within a society.

Is equality always justice?

Not necessarily. Some theories distinguish equality from equity and argue that fair treatment may require considering relevant differences in circumstances.

How should a society distribute its benefits and burdens?

This question, known as distributive justice, has produced some of the most influential and sharply opposed theories in modern political philosophy. Philosopher John Rawls proposed imagining people designing the rules of society from behind a "veil of ignorance," a hypothetical position in which no one knows what social class, talents, or circumstances they'll end up with. Rawls argued that people reasoning this way, unable to rig the rules in their own favor, would agree to protect basic liberties for everyone and would only accept inequalities in wealth or opportunity if those inequalities actually worked to benefit society's least advantaged members, an idea he called the difference principle. Philosopher Robert Nozick offered a very different answer through his entitlement theory: a distribution is just when people are entitled to what they possess through just acquisition and voluntary transfer, with injustices in past acquisition or transfer requiring rectification. For Nozick, redistributing wealth simply to make outcomes more equal can itself violate people's rights over what they are entitled to keep. These two views, along with a range of others in between, remain a central and unresolved dividing line in political philosophy.

What about justice after a wrong has been done?

A separate branch of the topic, sometimes called corrective or criminal justice, asks what should happen once someone has committed a wrong. Retributive justice holds that offenders deserve a penalty proportionate to their offense, treating punishment as justified partly because a wrongdoer deserves a proportionate response. Retributive ideas have deep historical roots, appearing in various ancient legal and philosophical traditions. Restorative justice takes a different approach, focusing less on punishment and more on repairing the harm done, often through a process that brings the offender, the victim, and the community together to address the aftermath directly, for example through restitution, an apology, or community service rather than, or alongside, formal punishment. Many contemporary justice systems and programs incorporate elements of both approaches rather than relying exclusively on one.

Is justice only about outcomes, or also about the process?

Some philosophers separate what's called procedural justice, whether the process used to reach a decision was fair, transparent, and applied consistently, from distributive or outcome-based justice, whether the actual result was fair. On this distinction, a fair coin flip used to decide something can be procedurally just even if one side ends up disappointed by the outcome, while a rigged process can produce an outcome that happens to look fair by luck but was reached unjustly. Many real-world debates about justice, in courtrooms, workplaces, or public policy, turn as much on whether the process was conducted fairly as on whether people are satisfied with the final result.

In short

Justice covers more ground than a single principle can capture: how a society should divide its benefits and burdens, how it should respond once someone has done wrong, and whether its decision-making processes are themselves fair. Philosophers disagree sharply on the details, particularly on questions of distributive justice, where views like Rawls's and Nozick's start from very different assumptions about individual rights and collective responsibility and arrive at very different conclusions. That disagreement is part of why justice remains one of the most actively contested concepts in philosophy and public life alike.
