What Is Knowledge?

Philosophy

Epistemology examines knowledge, including what it means to know something and how beliefs can be justified.

Is true belief enough for knowledge?

Many traditional accounts distinguish knowledge from merely having a true belief by requiring some form of justification or reliable basis.

What is the classic definition of knowledge?

For much of the history of philosophy, going back to ideas discussed by Plato, knowledge was commonly analyzed as "justified true belief," often shortened to JTB. On this view, a person knows a claim if, and only if, three conditions are met: they believe the claim, the claim is actually true, and they have good justification, such as evidence or a reliable reason, for believing it. A lucky guess that happens to be correct wouldn't count as knowledge under this definition, since it's true but not justified, and a well-reasoned belief that turns out to be false wouldn't count either, since it's justified but not true.

Can you be justified, believe something true, and still not know it?

In 1963, philosopher Edmund Gettier published a short, highly influential paper arguing that justified true belief isn't actually sufficient for knowledge. His counterexamples describe situations where someone forms a belief that turns out to be both justified and true, but only because of a lucky coincidence rather than because their justification genuinely connected them to the truth. A commonly used illustration of this same pattern, originally devised by philosopher Bertrand Russell over a decade before Gettier's paper: imagine glancing at a clock that reads 2 o'clock and forming the justified belief that it's 2 o'clock, when in fact it happens to actually be 2 o'clock, but only because the clock is broken and permanently stuck at that time. The belief is true, and looking at a clock is normally a justified way to tell the time, yet most people's intuition is that this doesn't count as genuinely knowing what time it is, since the belief being correct was essentially an accident. Cases like this became known as "Gettier problems," and they show that a belief can satisfy justification and truth while still missing something knowledge seems to require.

How have philosophers tried to fix the definition?

Since Gettier's paper, epistemologists have proposed various additional conditions meant to rule out these accidental, lucky cases, such as requiring that a belief not depend on any false step in its reasoning, or that it be produced by a reliable process that reliably tracks the truth. Each proposed fix has tended to run into new counterexamples of its own, leading some philosophers, most notably Linda Zagzebski, to argue that any definition built by simply adding conditions onto justified true belief will always remain vulnerable to some Gettier-style case. As a result, there's still no single, universally accepted definition of knowledge, even though the JTB framework remains a useful and widely taught starting point.

How do we know anything about the world at all?

A separate, more radical epistemological question asks not how to precisely define knowledge, but whether we can be certain of anything at all. The philosopher René Descartes explored this by imagining an all-powerful "evil demon" bent on deceiving him about everything, including the existence of the physical world and even basic mathematical truths. Taking this doubt as far as it could go, Descartes searched for something that would remain certain even under such extreme deception, and concluded that the very act of doubting proved at least one thing: that he existed as a thinking being, summarized in his famous phrase "I think, therefore I am." Modern versions of this same puzzle include the "brain in a vat" scenario and the idea of living inside a simulated reality, both of which ask the same underlying question Descartes raised centuries earlier: how would you ever be able to tell the difference between a genuine perception of reality and a perfectly convincing illusion of one?

In short

Philosophers have long tried to pin down exactly what separates knowledge from a lucky guess or a mistaken belief. The traditional answer, justified true belief, works well in ordinary cases but breaks down in cleverly constructed edge cases uncovered by Gettier, and no replacement definition has managed to escape the problem entirely. Beneath that debate sits an even deeper question, raised by Descartes and still discussed today, about whether we can ever be fully certain that our beliefs about the world are accurate at all, rather than simply the most convincing illusion available to us.
