What Makes Something Right or Wrong?

Philosophy

Ethics asks how we should decide what is right and wrong. Some approaches focus on consequences, some on duties and principles, and others on character and virtues.

Is there one universal answer?

Philosophers disagree. The disagreement exists partly because moral reasoning can involve competing values, such as freedom, fairness, harm, responsibility, and compassion.

What do consequences-focused theories say?

Consequentialist theories judge an action by its outcomes rather than by the intentions behind it or the rules it follows. The best-known version is utilitarianism, developed by philosophers including Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, which holds that the right action is the one that produces the greatest overall happiness or well-being for the greatest number of people affected. Under this view, an act that seems harsh in isolation, such as telling someone an uncomfortable truth, can still be the right one if it leads to a better overall outcome than the alternatives. Critics argue that a pure focus on outcomes can, in principle, justify treating individuals unfairly if doing so produces a greater total benefit for everyone else.

What do duty-focused theories say?

Deontological theories judge an action by whether it follows the correct moral rule or duty, regardless of the consequences that follow. The most influential version comes from the German philosopher Immanuel Kant, who argued that a person should only act according to a principle they could rationally will to become a universal law that everyone follows, an idea he called the categorical imperative. For Kant, this meant certain acts, like lying or treating another person merely as a tool for one's own purposes, are wrong in themselves, even if breaking the rule would produce a better outcome in a specific case. Critics argue that rigid rules can produce results that feel wrong in unusual or extreme situations where following the rule causes clear harm.

What do character-focused theories say?

Virtue ethics, most associated with the ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle, takes a different starting point altogether. Rather than asking "what rule should I follow?" or "what outcome should I aim for?", it asks "what would a person of good character do?" On this view, morality is less about isolated actions and more about the ongoing cultivation of traits like honesty, courage, and fairness through practice, in the same way a skill is built through repetition. A virtuous person is expected to act well somewhat naturally, because their character has been shaped that way, rather than by consulting a rule or calculating an outcome in the moment. Critics point out that virtue ethics can be vague about what to do in a specific hard case, since "act as a virtuous person would" doesn't always translate into a concrete instruction.

Where do our moral intuitions actually come from?

Separately from the philosophical debate about which theory is correct, psychologists have studied where everyday moral judgments actually come from in the mind. Social psychologist Jonathan Haidt has argued that most moral judgments arise quickly and intuitively, more like a gut reaction than a chain of logical reasoning, with conscious reasoning often arriving afterward to justify a conclusion the mind had already reached. Building on this, Haidt and colleagues developed moral foundations theory, which proposes that human morality rests on a small set of shared intuitive foundations, including care versus harm, fairness versus cheating, and loyalty versus betrayal, on top of which different cultures and communities build their own specific values and rules. This research doesn't settle which ethical theory is philosophically correct, but it offers one account of why moral disagreements can feel so immediate and emotionally charged rather than purely analytical.

Is morality something we discover, or something we invent?

Underneath the disagreement between specific theories sits a deeper question: are moral truths real and objective, waiting to be discovered the way scientific facts are, or are they constructed by individuals, cultures, or societies, meaning they could have turned out differently? Moral realists argue that some things really are right or wrong independent of what anyone happens to believe. Moral relativists and constructivists argue that moral standards are created by human agreement, culture, or convention, and can legitimately vary between societies or historical periods. Both positions have serious, long-standing philosophical defenders, and the debate remains one of the most fundamental open questions in ethics.

In short

There's no single formula philosophers have agreed on for telling right from wrong. Some focus on the consequences of an action, some on the duties and rules behind it, and some on the character of the person acting. Psychological research suggests that, in practice, many moral judgments start as fast intuitions rather than careful calculations, and philosophers still disagree about whether morality is something objective waiting to be found or something people and cultures construct for themselves. The disagreement itself is part of why ethics remains an active and unsettled field rather than a solved one.
