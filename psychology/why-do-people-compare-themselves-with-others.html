Why Do People Compare Themselves With Others?

Psychology

Social comparison can help people evaluate their abilities, progress, and social position. It may also influence motivation and self-evaluation.

Why do people compare themselves to others in the first place?

A long-standing idea in psychology, known as social comparison theory, proposes that people have a basic drive to evaluate their own opinions and abilities. When there is no objective, external standard available, no ruler to measure how funny, capable, or successful someone is, people turn to other people as a reference point instead. This is one reason comparison tends to increase in unfamiliar or ambiguous situations, such as starting a new job or an unfamiliar social setting, where a person has little other information to judge themselves against.

What's the difference between upward and downward comparison?

Researchers generally distinguish between upward comparisons, made with someone perceived as better off in some way, and downward comparisons, made with someone perceived as worse off. Upward comparisons can be motivating, seeing someone excel at something can clarify what's possible and inspire effort, but they are also more consistently linked to feelings of inadequacy, envy, or lowered self-esteem, especially when the comparison feels unreachable. Downward comparisons, by contrast, are more often associated with a temporary boost in how someone feels about themselves, though relying on them too heavily can also limit personal growth.

Why does social media change how people compare themselves?

Social media has expanded how often, and how selectively, people encounter comparison targets. Feeds are often filled with edited highlights of other people's lives, achievements, good days, flattering photos, which skews the available comparison material upward. Several recent studies have linked frequent upward comparison on social media to lower self-esteem and reduced wellbeing, particularly among younger users, and found that people with already lower self-esteem tend to make more frequent and more extreme upward comparisons while browsing, which can compound the effect.

Can comparison be harmful?

Yes. Frequent unfavourable comparisons can undermine confidence, particularly when people compare themselves with idealised or incomplete portrayals of others. Because the comparisons that come easiest online are often with people's curated best moments rather than their full, ordinary lives, the comparison itself can be distorted from the start: closer to comparing an average day to someone else's highlight reel than to a fair, like-for-like evaluation.
