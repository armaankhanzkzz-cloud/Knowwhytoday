Why Do Objects Fall Toward Earth?

Science

Objects near Earth fall because Earth’s gravity attracts them toward its centre. Gravity is a pulling force that exists between any two masses, and Earth is massive enough that its pull noticeably speeds up anything nearby. The direction it pulls in, how fast things accelerate, and why some objects seem to defy it all come down to a small set of ideas that are easy to understand once laid out clearly.

What is gravity, exactly?

Gravity is an attractive force that exists between any two objects that have mass, not just between Earth and things on it. Every object with mass pulls on every other object with mass, including you and the device you are reading this on right now. The strength of that pull depends on how much mass the two objects have and how far apart they are: more mass means a stronger pull, and greater distance means a weaker one.

Earth’s pull feels significant to us because Earth is enormous compared with everyday objects. The mutual attraction between you and, say, a coffee mug technically exists, but it is far too small to notice. Earth’s mass is billions of times larger, so its gravitational pull is the one that dominates everyday experience.

Why do objects fall toward Earth’s centre specifically?

Gravity pulls objects toward the centre of mass of the attracting body. For a roughly spherical planet like Earth, that centre of mass is very close to the geometric centre of the sphere. This is why "down" always means toward Earth’s centre, no matter where you are standing on the globe.

This also explains why people on opposite sides of the planet do not fall off. Someone standing in Australia and someone standing in the United Kingdom both experience "down" as the direction toward Earth’s centre, which means they are both being pulled toward the ground beneath their own feet, not toward some single fixed direction in space.

Do heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones?

In a vacuum, no. This is one of the more surprising results in basic physics: a heavy object and a light object, dropped from the same height with no air present, hit the ground at exactly the same time. A greater mass does experience a stronger gravitational force, but it also has more inertia, meaning more resistance to changing its motion, and these two effects cancel out exactly, leaving the acceleration the same for any mass.

This was famously demonstrated on the Moon during the Apollo 15 mission, when astronaut David Scott dropped a geology hammer and a falcon feather at the same time. With no atmosphere to get in the way, both objects struck the lunar surface simultaneously, just as Galileo had argued centuries earlier.

Then why does a feather fall more slowly here on Earth?

Air resistance, not gravity, is responsible. As an object falls through air, it has to push air molecules out of the way, and this creates a drag force that opposes the fall. A feather has a lot of surface area relative to its weight, so air resistance affects it strongly and slows it down. A hammer or a rock has much less surface area relative to its weight, so air resistance barely affects it at all.

Eventually, a falling object’s speed can increase until the air resistance pushing up on it exactly balances the pull of gravity pulling it down. At that point the object stops accelerating and falls at a constant speed, known as terminal velocity. Light, spread-out objects like feathers or parachutes reach a low terminal velocity quickly; dense, compact objects reach a much higher terminal velocity, or may hit the ground before reaching it at all.

What is the difference between mass and weight?

Mass is a measure of how much matter an object contains, and it does not change no matter where the object is located. Weight is the force that gravity exerts on that mass, and it does change depending on location, because it depends on the strength of the local gravitational pull.

An astronaut has the same mass on Earth and on the Moon, but weighs about one-sixth as much on the Moon, because the Moon’s mass, and therefore its gravitational pull, is much smaller than Earth’s. This is also why objects on the Moon fall more slowly than they would on Earth, even though the underlying rule (heavier and lighter objects falling at the same rate in the absence of air resistance) still holds in both places.

Does the Moon fall toward Earth too?

Yes, constantly. This is one of the more elegant ideas in physics, first worked out by Isaac Newton. Imagine firing a cannonball horizontally from a very tall tower. A slow cannonball curves downward and lands not far from the tower. A faster cannonball travels further before it curves down and lands. If the cannonball is fired fast enough, something different happens: it falls toward Earth at the same rate that Earth’s curved surface falls away beneath it. The result is that the cannonball never gets any closer to the ground. It keeps "falling" all the way around the planet. That is exactly what an orbit is.

The Moon is doing precisely this around Earth, just as Earth does it around the Sun, and as satellites do when engineers place them in orbit. None of these objects have "escaped" gravity; they are all falling, just with enough sideways speed that they continually miss the body they are falling toward.

Is gravity exactly the same everywhere on Earth?

Not quite, though the differences are small enough that you would never notice them day to day. Gravity is very slightly weaker at higher altitudes, since you are further from Earth’s centre, and very slightly weaker near the equator than near the poles, partly because Earth bulges outward at the equator and partly because of the effect of Earth’s rotation. Local variations in the density of rock beneath the surface can also cause tiny differences. These effects are measured by scientific instruments but are far too small to affect anything in ordinary life.

In short

Objects fall toward Earth because gravity is a pulling force between any two masses, and Earth’s enormous mass makes that pull impossible to ignore. That pull always points toward Earth’s centre, which is why "down" means something different depending on where you are standing on the globe. In a vacuum, all objects fall at the same rate regardless of mass; the differences we see in everyday life come from air resistance acting differently on different shapes. And the same falling motion that pulls an apple to the ground also keeps the Moon, satellites, and planets in orbit: they are all falling, just with enough sideways motion to keep missing the thing they are falling toward.
