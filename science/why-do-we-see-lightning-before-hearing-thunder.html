Why Do We See Lightning Before Hearing Thunder?

Science

Lightning and thunder are produced by the same storm event, but light travels through air enormously faster than sound.

Why is there a delay?

The flash reaches your eyes almost immediately, while the sound wave from the lightning takes longer to travel through the atmosphere. The longer the delay, the farther away the lightning is.

How fast is each signal actually moving?

Light travels at roughly 300,000 kilometers (186,000 miles) per second, fast enough that a flash from a storm a few kilometers away effectively reaches your eyes the instant it happens. Sound is far slower and far more affected by the air it moves through, traveling at only about 343 meters (1,125 feet) per second at typical ground-level temperatures. That works out to roughly one kilometer every three seconds, or one mile every five seconds. The gap between the two speeds is enormous: light outruns sound by nearly a million times over, which is why the flash and the rumble that clearly belong to the same event can feel like two separate happenings.

Where does the sound of thunder come from?

Thunder isn't a separate event from lightning, it's the direct byproduct of it. A lightning bolt heats the narrow channel of air it passes through to roughly 30,000 Kelvin, several times hotter than the surface of the sun, in a fraction of a second. That air expands violently outward, and the resulting shockwave is what we hear as thunder. Because a lightning bolt can stretch for several kilometers through the sky, different segments of the channel are different distances from the listener. Sound from the closest part arrives first as a sharp crack, while sound from farther, higher segments arrives later and is scattered by the atmosphere along the way, which is why thunder trails off into a rolling rumble rather than ending in a single sharp bang.

Using the delay to estimate distance

Because the speed of sound in air is so predictable, the gap between flash and rumble can be used as a rough ruler. Counting the seconds from the moment you see the flash to the moment you hear the thunder and dividing by five gives the distance to the strike in miles; dividing by three gives the distance in kilometers. A five-second gap, for instance, points to a strike about one mile (1.6 kilometers) away. Shrinking gaps over successive flashes mean a storm is moving closer, which is useful information for deciding when to head indoors, since lightning can strike from several miles ahead of a storm's leading edge, well before rain or visible clouds arrive overhead.

Why don't the two events line up when they're that close?

Even at fairly short distances, the mismatch in speed adds up quickly. A strike just two miles away produces a delay of about ten seconds, long enough to feel like a distinct pause rather than a rounding error. It's a helpful reminder that our senses don't always register simultaneity accurately. The lightning and the thunder happen at essentially the same instant at the source, and it's only the very different travel times of light and sound through air that stretch that single event into two separate sensory experiences.
