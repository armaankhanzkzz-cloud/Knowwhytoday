Why Does the Sun Look Red at Sunset?

Science

The Sun can look pale yellow or almost white during the day, yet turn orange or deep red as it approaches the horizon. The Sun itself has not suddenly changed color. The difference comes from the much longer journey sunlight takes through Earth's atmosphere when the Sun is low in the sky.

Why does the Sun look different at sunset?

Sunlight contains a mixture of visible colors. When the Sun is high in the sky, its light reaches us after passing through a comparatively short path through the atmosphere. Near sunset, the Sun is close to the horizon, so its light travels through a much longer path before reaching an observer.

That longer journey gives sunlight more opportunities to interact with molecules and tiny particles in the atmosphere. Some wavelengths are scattered away from the original path more strongly than others. As a result, the mixture of colors in the direct sunlight changes by the time it reaches our eyes.

What happens to the blue light?

As sunlight travels through Earth's atmosphere, shorter wavelengths such as blue are scattered more strongly than longer wavelengths such as red. This is the same basic atmospheric scattering that makes the daytime sky appear blue, which we explain in more detail in our article Why Is the Sky Blue?

At sunset, however, the important question is what happens to the direct beam of sunlight. Because that beam has travelled through much more atmosphere, more of its shorter-wavelength light has been redirected away from the original path. The light that continues toward the observer becomes richer in longer wavelengths.

Why does the Sun become orange and then red?

The change is gradual rather than sudden. When the Sun is still relatively high, the direct sunlight contains a broad mixture of visible wavelengths and the Sun can appear pale yellow or golden.

As the Sun gets lower, the atmospheric path becomes longer. More of the shorter-wavelength light is scattered out of the direct beam, leaving proportionally more yellow, orange, and red light. Very close to the horizon, the Sun can therefore appear strongly orange or red.

The Sun has not changed its physical color. The atmosphere has changed the mixture of sunlight that reaches your eyes.

Why is the path through the atmosphere longer?

Earth's atmosphere surrounds the planet in a broad layer. When the Sun is high overhead, sunlight reaches the surface through a relatively direct route through that layer.

When the Sun is near the horizon, sunlight travels through the atmosphere at a shallow angle. It must pass through a much greater amount of atmosphere before reaching an observer. That extra distance is one of the most important reasons the sunset Sun looks different from the midday Sun.

Why does the whole sky become orange or red?

The red Sun and the colorful sunset sky are closely related, but they are not exactly the same visual effect. The Sun is the direct source of the light reaching your eyes along one particular path. At the same time, sunlight is being scattered in many directions through the atmosphere.

Near sunset, the balance of colors reaching different parts of the sky can produce broad areas of yellow, orange, pink, and red. Clouds can make these colors even more noticeable because they can reflect and scatter sunlight toward an observer.

Why can clouds become bright orange or red?

Clouds do not need to create the red color themselves. They can act as surfaces that receive sunlight and scatter it toward us.

A cloud near the horizon can be illuminated by sunlight that has already travelled through a long atmospheric path. Because that sunlight contains a greater proportion of warm colors, the cloud can appear golden, orange, or red.

High clouds can sometimes remain illuminated after the Sun has disappeared from the horizon because they can still receive sunlight from above the surface. This is why dramatic colors can continue for a while after the solar disk is no longer visible.

Why are some sunsets much more colorful than others?

Earth's atmosphere is not identical every day. It contains gases as well as varying amounts of water droplets, dust, smoke, and other tiny particles. Their amount, size, composition, and altitude can affect how sunlight is scattered and transmitted.

This means that two sunsets viewed from the same location can look very different. One evening may produce a soft golden glow, while another may produce strong orange and red colors.

Does pollution always make a sunset more red?

No. It is easy to hear that pollution or smoke automatically creates beautiful red sunsets, but the situation is more complicated.

Some atmospheric particles can contribute to striking colors under the right conditions. However, heavy haze close to the ground can scatter light in many directions and make the view paler or less distinct. Simply having more pollution does not guarantee a more colorful sunset.

The appearance depends on the type of particles, where they are located, how much is present, and how sunlight passes through the atmosphere.

Is the Sun actually cooler when it looks red?

No. The red appearance should not be interpreted as the Sun suddenly becoming cooler. The atmosphere is changing the light that reaches an observer by scattering and transmitting different wavelengths in different proportions.

So the red appearance tells us about the journey of sunlight through Earth's atmosphere, not a sudden change in the Sun itself.

How is this connected to the blue sky?

The two questions are connected by the same basic atmospheric scattering process.

In the blue-sky explanation, the important observation is that scattered blue light reaches our eyes from many directions. At sunset, we are especially interested in the direct sunlight coming from the low Sun. Because that light has travelled through a much longer atmospheric path, more of its shorter-wavelength light has been scattered away before the beam reaches us.

So these are two sides of the same phenomenon: the blue daytime sky is strongly influenced by scattered shorter-wavelength light, while the setting Sun's direct light becomes richer in longer wavelengths after travelling through a long atmospheric path.

In short

The Sun looks red at sunset because its light travels through a much longer path in Earth's atmosphere than it does when the Sun is high in the sky.

Along that longer path, shorter wavelengths such as blue are scattered away from the direct beam more strongly than longer wavelengths such as red and orange. The sunlight that reaches our eyes is therefore richer in longer wavelengths, making the Sun appear increasingly orange or red.

The Sun itself has not changed color. The atmosphere has changed the mixture of sunlight reaching us.

So when you watch a deep red Sun disappear below the horizon, you are seeing the result of sunlight taking its longest journey through Earth's atmosphere before reaching your eyes.
