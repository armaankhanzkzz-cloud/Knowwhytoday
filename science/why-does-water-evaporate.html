Why Does Water Evaporate?

Science

A puddle can disappear even when the water never boils. That happens because water molecules are always moving, and some molecules at the surface have enough energy to escape into the air as water vapour. Evaporation is one of the everyday processes that keeps Earth's water cycle moving.

Does water need to boil to evaporate?

No. Boiling and evaporation are both ways for liquid water to become a gas, but they happen differently. Boiling occurs throughout a liquid when conditions allow bubbles of water vapour to form. Evaporation happens at the surface and can occur at temperatures well below the boiling point.

What is happening to the water molecules?

Water may look still, but its molecules are constantly moving. They do not all have exactly the same amount of kinetic energy at any moment. Some are moving faster than others.

At the surface of a body of water, a molecule that is moving fast enough can escape the attractive forces holding it among the other water molecules. Once it leaves the liquid, it becomes water vapour and mixes with the surrounding air.

This does not mean that every molecule near the surface escapes. Only a fraction have the right combination of energy and position to leave at a particular moment. At the same time, water-vapour molecules in the air can return to the liquid surface. Evaporation is therefore part of a continual exchange between liquid water and water vapour.

Why does heat make evaporation faster?

Heating water gives its molecules more energy on average. That increases the number of molecules that can escape from the surface, so evaporation generally becomes faster as the temperature of the water rises.

This is why a wet surface often dries faster in warm conditions. The Sun is an important source of energy for evaporation across Earth's surface, helping move water from oceans, lakes, rivers, and wet ground into the atmosphere.

Why does wind help a puddle dry?

Temperature is not the only factor that matters. Air moving across a wet surface can carry water vapour away from the area immediately above the water. This makes it easier for additional water molecules to enter the air.

That is why a breeze can speed up the drying of wet clothes or a puddle. The wind is not directly “pulling” individual molecules out of the water. Instead, it changes the air around the surface and helps transport the water vapour away.

What does humidity have to do with evaporation?

The amount of water vapour already present in the surrounding air also matters. Air that is relatively dry has more capacity to receive additional water vapour than air that is already very humid.

As a result, evaporation can be slower when the air near a wet surface is already close to saturation. In dry air, water vapour can usually be added more readily. This is one reason the same wet towel can dry at different rates under different weather conditions.

Why does evaporation make things feel cooler?

Evaporation can have a cooling effect because the molecules that escape from a liquid carry energy with them. The liquid that remains can therefore lose some of its thermal energy.

You can notice this effect when water evaporates from your skin. When conditions allow evaporation to happen readily, the process can remove heat from the skin and make you feel cooler. Sweating uses this same physical principle: the cooling comes mainly from sweat evaporating rather than simply from sweat being present on the skin.

Where does the evaporated water go?

Once water has evaporated, it is present in the atmosphere as water vapour, an invisible gas. Water vapour can move with the atmosphere, and when moist air cools under suitable conditions, the vapour can condense into tiny liquid droplets.

Those droplets can contribute to clouds. Eventually, water can return to Earth's surface as precipitation such as rain or snow. Evaporation, condensation, and precipitation are therefore connected parts of the water cycle.

Is evaporation happening everywhere?

Evaporation occurs from many kinds of wet surfaces, including oceans, lakes, rivers, soil, and water held on vegetation. Oceans are especially important because they cover such a large portion of Earth's surface. NASA explains that evaporation from oceans and other bodies of water supplies most of the moisture that enters the atmosphere, while plants add water through a related process called transpiration.

Evaporation does not require a sunny day, either. Sunlight is an important energy source, but evaporation can occur whenever water molecules at a surface have enough energy to enter the gas phase. The rate changes depending on conditions such as temperature, wind, humidity, and the surrounding environment.

Why is evaporation important?

Evaporation is much more than a reason for a puddle to disappear. It is a major part of the movement of water around Earth. Water moves from the surface into the atmosphere, travels with air currents, and can later return to the surface through condensation and precipitation.

Evaporation also moves energy around the Earth system. The energy absorbed when liquid water changes into vapour is carried with that water vapour. When the vapour later condenses, energy is released into the surrounding atmosphere. This links the water cycle with weather and Earth's broader energy cycle.

In short

Water evaporates because its molecules are constantly moving. Some molecules at the surface have enough energy to overcome the forces keeping them in the liquid and escape as water vapour. Evaporation can happen below the boiling point, and it generally becomes faster when water is warmer, when moving air carries vapour away, or when the surrounding air is relatively dry.

That simple process is happening all around us. A drying puddle, a wet towel, moisture leaving a lake, and water entering the atmosphere from the ocean are all examples of the same physical process at work.
