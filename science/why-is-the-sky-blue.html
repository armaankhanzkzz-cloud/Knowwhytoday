Why Is the Sky Blue?

Science

Look up on a clear day and the answer seems obvious: the sky is blue. But sunlight itself looks white. So where does that blue come from? The answer lies in the way sunlight interacts with the tiny molecules that make up Earth's atmosphere.

Sunlight is made of many colors

Sunlight may look like a single white beam, but visible sunlight is a mixture of many colors. Each color corresponds to a different wavelength. Red light has a relatively long wavelength, while blue and violet light have shorter wavelengths. When all of these visible colors reach our eyes together, they can appear white.

What happens when sunlight enters the atmosphere?

Before sunlight reaches the ground, it has to pass through Earth's atmosphere. The atmosphere is made mostly of gases, including nitrogen and oxygen. These molecules are incredibly small compared with the wavelengths of visible light.

Light normally travels in a straight line, but when sunlight interacts with molecules in the atmosphere, some of it is redirected in different directions. This process is called scattering. The particular kind of scattering caused by particles much smaller than the wavelength of light is known as Rayleigh scattering.

Why is blue scattered more than red?

Rayleigh scattering depends strongly on wavelength. Shorter wavelengths are scattered much more efficiently than longer wavelengths. In a clear atmosphere, blue light is therefore redirected much more often than red light. NASA notes that blue light is scattered much more strongly than red light.

That scattered blue light reaches our eyes from many parts of the sky. Instead of seeing only the direct path of sunlight, we receive blue light that has been redirected by air molecules. This is why a clear daytime sky appears blue even when we are looking away from the Sun.

Then why isn't the sky violet?

Violet has an even shorter wavelength than blue, so it is scattered efficiently too. Yet the sky does not usually look violet to us. Part of the explanation is that sunlight contains less violet than some of the neighboring colors, some violet light is absorbed in the upper atmosphere, and human eyes are more sensitive to blue than violet. The combined effect makes blue the dominant color we perceive.

Why are sunsets red and orange?

The same scattering process explains one of the most beautiful changes in the sky. When the Sun is high overhead, its light takes a relatively short path through the atmosphere before reaching us. At sunrise and sunset, the Sun is close to the horizon, so its light travels through a much longer stretch of atmosphere.

Along that longer path, much of the blue and violet light is scattered away from the direct beam. The remaining direct sunlight is therefore richer in longer-wavelength colors such as red and orange. Dust, smoke, pollution, and other aerosols can also influence the colors and intensity of a sunset.

Why can the sky look different on some days?

A clear sky is mainly a Rayleigh-scattering story, but the atmosphere contains more than just gas molecules. Larger particles such as dust, smoke, pollution, and water droplets can scatter light in different ways. These particles can make the sky look hazier, paler, or more intensely colored during some sunrises and sunsets.

What about the sky from space?

From space, there is no thick atmosphere surrounding an observer in the same way there is on Earth's surface. With far fewer molecules available to scatter sunlight toward an observer, the sky around the Sun appears dark rather than blue. The blue color of our daytime sky is therefore not the color of space itself; it is a visible consequence of sunlight interacting with Earth's atmosphere.

In short

The sky is blue because sunlight contains many colors, and Earth's atmosphere scatters the shorter wavelengths more strongly than the longer ones. Blue light is scattered throughout the atmosphere and reaches our eyes from many directions. At sunrise and sunset, sunlight travels through more atmosphere, so more of the blue light is scattered out of the direct path and the remaining light looks warmer—often red, orange, or yellow.
