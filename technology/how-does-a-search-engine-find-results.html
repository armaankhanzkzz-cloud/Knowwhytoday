How Does a Search Engine Find Results?

Technology

Search engines generally discover webpages, analyse their content, store information in an index, and use ranking systems to select relevant results for a query.

Why do different searches show different results?

Ranking can depend on the query, relevance signals, freshness, language, location, and other factors.

How does a search engine first find a webpage?

Before a page can ever show up in results, it has to be found. Search engines use automated programs called crawlers, sometimes called spiders or bots, that constantly move around the web, following links from one page to another the way a person might click from link to link. When a crawler visits a page, it makes note of what's there and also looks for any new links on that page, which it can then follow to discover more pages it hasn't seen yet. This is why a brand new website usually can't appear in search results until it's been linked to from somewhere a crawler already visits, or submitted directly for crawling.

What happens after a page is found?

Once a page has been crawled, the search engine needs to actually understand it, a step called indexing. During indexing, the content of the page is analyzed and stored in a huge, organized database called the index, similar in spirit to the catalog system a library uses to keep track of every book it owns. The search engine notes what topics and keywords the page covers, how it's structured, and when it was last updated, so that all of this can be quickly referenced later. Not every page that gets crawled ends up in the index. Pages that are blocked from crawling, extremely low quality, or otherwise unsuitable can be skipped, which is one reason a page might exist on the web but never appear in search results.

Why doesn't a search happen on the live web itself?

When someone types in a search, the engine isn't scanning billions of live websites in real time, which would be far too slow to return an answer in a fraction of a second. Instead, it searches its own pre-built index, the massive database it already assembled through crawling and indexing, and pulls out the pages that seem to match the query.

How does a search engine decide what to show first?

Matching a query to relevant pages is only part of the job, since a search engine also has to decide which of those matching pages to show first. This final step is called ranking. One of the earliest and most famous ranking methods, called PageRank, was developed by Google's founders and worked by treating each link from one page to another as a kind of vote of confidence, with links from already well-regarded pages counting for more than links from obscure ones. Modern search engines have expanded far beyond that original idea, now weighing hundreds of different signals together, including how well a page matches the specific meaning behind a query, how fast the page loads, whether it works well on mobile devices, how trustworthy and well-established the source appears to be, and how up to date the content is. This is why search results can shift for the exact same query over time, and why two people searching the same phrase in different locations, or in different languages, can end up seeing noticeably different results.

In short

Finding a search result is really the end of a three-step process. Crawling discovers pages by following links across the web, indexing analyzes and stores what those pages contain, and ranking decides which of the matching pages in that index deserve to appear first for a given search. Each step depends on the one before it, which is why a page has to be found and understood before it can ever have a chance at ranking well.
