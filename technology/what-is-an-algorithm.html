What Is an Algorithm?

Technology

An algorithm is a defined sequence of steps for solving a problem or completing a computational task.

Why are algorithms important?

Algorithms provide systematic procedures that computers can execute efficiently and consistently. Rather than figuring out a solution from scratch every time, a well-designed algorithm captures a proven method once so it can be reused reliably, whether that's sorting a list, finding the fastest driving route, or deciding which post to show next in a social media feed.

What exactly counts as an algorithm?

Not every set of instructions qualifies as an algorithm in the formal sense. Computer scientist Donald Knuth, in his influential textbook "The Art of Computer Programming," described an algorithm as needing five properties: it must have defined inputs and produce a defined output, every step must be precise and unambiguous rather than open to interpretation, each individual step must be simple enough to actually be carried out, and, critically, the whole process must terminate after a finite number of steps rather than running forever. A recipe that says "add a dash of salt" fails the unambiguous-step test, while a process that could loop indefinitely without ever finishing fails the finiteness test, which is why computer scientists distinguish a true algorithm from a looser "computational method" or "heuristic."

Where does the word "algorithm" come from?

The word traces back to the name of the ninth-century Persian mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi, who worked at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad during the Islamic Golden Age. His writings on Hindu-Arabic numerals and systematic methods for solving equations were translated into Latin centuries later, and his Latinized name, "Algoritmi," gradually morphed into "algorismus" and eventually the modern word "algorithm." The same man who gave us "algorithm" also gave us "algebra," since it derives from "al-jabr," one of the operations al-Khwarizmi described for solving equations, meaning both words ultimately trace back to a single ninth-century text.

What's the difference between an algorithm and a computer program?

An algorithm is a general method for solving a problem, described independently of any particular programming language, the way a recipe describes a dish independently of which kitchen it's cooked in. A program is one specific implementation of that method, written in a language like Python or C++, that a computer can actually run. The same algorithm, such as a standard method for sorting a list of numbers, can be implemented as many different programs across different languages, and a single problem can often be solved by several genuinely different algorithms, some faster or more memory-efficient than others, even though all of them produce the same correct result.

Why do some algorithms work faster than others?

Computer scientists compare algorithms using a field called the analysis of algorithms, which asks how the time or memory an algorithm needs grows as its input gets larger, rather than just measuring how fast it happens to run on one particular computer. This growth rate is often summarized using a notation called Big O, which describes, in broad strokes, whether doubling the size of the input roughly doubles the work, roughly squares it, or barely changes it at all. Two algorithms that produce identical, correct answers can behave very differently at scale, one might handle a list of a thousand items instantly while the other becomes impractically slow, which is why choosing the right algorithm, not just writing correct code, is central to building software that performs well as it grows.

Are algorithms only for computers?

No; the concept predates computers by centuries and doesn't strictly require one at all. Knuth's own example of a classic algorithm, the Euclidean algorithm for finding the greatest common divisor of two numbers, was described by the ancient Greek mathematician Euclid long before anything resembling a modern computer existed, and any precise, step-by-step recipe followed reliably by hand, such as long division taught in school, technically counts as an algorithm too. What changed with computing wasn't the underlying idea, but the fact that algorithms could suddenly be executed automatically, at enormous speed and scale, which is also why the word has taken on a more loaded, sometimes controversial meaning today, as the algorithms behind search engines, recommendation feeds, and automated decisions increasingly shape what people see and how they're treated.

In short

An algorithm is a precise, finite sequence of steps for solving a problem, formally required to have clear inputs, outputs, unambiguous steps, and a guaranteed stopping point. The word itself comes from the Latinized name of a ninth-century Persian mathematician, long before anything like a modern computer existed. An algorithm is distinct from a program, which is one specific implementation of it, and different algorithms solving the very same problem can differ enormously in how efficiently they scale, which is why computer scientists study not just whether an algorithm works, but how well it holds up as the size of the problem grows.
