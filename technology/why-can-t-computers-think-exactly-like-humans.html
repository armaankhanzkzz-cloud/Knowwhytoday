Why Can't Computers Think Exactly Like Humans?

Technology

Computers can perform remarkable calculations and recognize complex patterns, but human intelligence involves perception, memory, emotions, social understanding, physical experience, and many other abilities.

What is the fundamental difference?

A computer follows computational processes designed by its software and learned parameters. Human cognition arises from a living biological system. Computers can imitate parts of human behavior without necessarily reproducing the full human experience.

How would we even test whether a computer can think?

In 1950, mathematician Alan Turing proposed a practical way to sidestep the difficult question of whether a machine can truly think. In what's now called the Turing Test, or the imitation game, a human judge holds text conversations with both a person and a computer, and tries to work out which is which. Turing suggested that if a computer could reliably fool the judge, that would be a reasonable, testable stand in for saying it can think, since he considered the deeper question of what thinking actually is too vague to settle directly. This test focuses entirely on outward behavior in conversation, which is exactly where much of the modern debate about computers and thinking still centers.

Does producing the right words mean a computer understands them?

Philosopher John Searle challenged the idea that passing a conversation test proves genuine understanding, using a thought experiment called the Chinese Room argument. Imagine a person who doesn't speak Chinese sitting in a room, receiving questions written in Chinese through a slot, and using a detailed instruction book to match each symbol to an appropriate response, which they then pass back out, all without understanding a single word. From outside the room, the responses might look fluent and convincing. Searle argued that this is essentially what a computer does when it processes language: it manipulates symbols according to rules without ever grasping what those symbols actually mean, so producing correct output isn't the same thing as understanding. Critics have pushed back with what's often called the systems reply, arguing that while the person in the room doesn't understand Chinese, the entire system, person plus instruction book plus room, arguably does, in some meaningful sense. The disagreement over what this thought experiment actually proves has continued for decades without a clear resolution.

What is the "symbol grounding problem"?

Related to Searle's argument, cognitive scientist Stevan Harnad raised what's known as the symbol grounding problem: how can a symbol inside a computer, like the word "apple," ever come to mean an actual apple, rather than just pointing to other symbols and definitions that are themselves ungrounded in anything real? For a human, the concept of an apple is tied to sensory experience: what it looks like, tastes like, feels like to hold. A computer working purely with text has traditionally lacked that direct sensory connection, relying instead on patterns learned from other text, which raises the question of whether its internal representations are meaningfully grounded in the world at all, or simply well organized symbols pointing at more symbols.

Why does physical experience matter so much to human thinking?

Many researchers who study embodied cognition argue that human thought can't be fully separated from having a body that moves through, senses, and acts on the physical world. On this view, concepts like balance, weight, effort, and even some emotional and social understanding are shaped by lived physical experience from infancy onward, not assembled purely from abstract information. A computer running software on a server, without a body, senses, or ongoing lived experience in the world, is missing much of the raw material human cognition is built from, which is part of why some researchers doubt it could reproduce human-style thinking through data and computation alone, no matter how sophisticated.

Has science actually settled this question?

No, and it's worth being clear about that rather than implying otherwise. Whether a sufficiently advanced computer could ever genuinely think, understand, or have subjective experience, rather than convincingly simulate the outward signs of it, remains a genuinely open question across philosophy, cognitive science, and artificial intelligence research, without anything close to expert consensus. Some researchers argue there's no principled reason a sufficiently sophisticated computational system couldn't eventually think in a meaningful sense, while others argue biological embodiment or some other special ingredient is required. That disagreement is a live, ongoing debate rather than a settled scientific fact in either direction.

In short

Computers can imitate impressive parts of human intelligence, holding fluent conversations, recognizing patterns, and solving complex problems, but whether that imitation amounts to genuine thinking, understanding, or experience remains deeply contested. Thought experiments like the Turing Test and the Chinese Room argument, along with ideas like the symbol grounding problem and embodied cognition, each highlight a different reason to be cautious about assuming a computer's fluent output reflects the same kind of inner life that produces human thought, even as the two can look remarkably similar from the outside.
